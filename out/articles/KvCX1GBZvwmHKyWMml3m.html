Web 3.0? More Like Web 2.5!

Last Modified 12/20/2022

What is Web 3.0?

    Before we delve into the details, we need to define Web 3.0. Web 3.0, as the name suggests, is the proposed third iteration of the internet. Web 1.0 roughly corresponds to the 1990 to 2005 era of the internet, which consists of static page websites. As the technology develops, people recognize that websites can do more, which brings about Web 2.0. The second iteration consists of centralized dynamic websites, allowing people to do anything that an app can do on a website. As many web-apps, part of Web 2.0, matures, concerns on privacy, data security, and centralization also manifested. Many websites collected user data without explicit consent, only to sell the data for profit. The centralized architecture employed by these websites proves to be an easier target to hackers since they only need to bypass a few security measures to access everything, as opposed to decentralized architecture, which requires hackers to bypass many securities in multiple systems. Lastly, the rise of big technology companies and its power are viewed as unfavorable by many, since only a small number of people (a selected number of employees) can ultimately make decisions on products that affect everyone and change the world. People seek to address those problems in Web 3.0, a proposed new iteration of the World Wide Web which incorporates blockchain technology. Now, multiple projects employing the technology are racing to replace and improve almost all web-apps in existence.

 

Concerns

    The third complete overhaul of the internet will not happen, for good reason. The three issues in Web 2.0, which Web 3.0 sets out to solve, were often not addressed in many Web 3.0 projects (at least in the current state).

Privacy and Security

    Web 3.0 does not offer as high a level of privacy and security as was often advertised. Public blockchains, by its nature, publicized everything on the chain. The same goes for many Web 3.0 related technologies like IPFS. This means that all transactions and activities associated with every account can be traced. The only barriers stopping the whole world from knowing what a degen you are when you buy LUNA are the lack of connection between you and the wallet address, proxies stopping direct links like wallet scrambling or cross chain connection, and encryption. There are still ways to trace the activities with enough persistence or parties with enough information to figure it all out – live wallet providers can have your real identity and wallet address. Proxies mentioned above are not particularly easy to use, and might take some technical expertise. Furthermore, since all pieces of information on the blockchain is public, you cannot put sensitive information on the chain. A simple mistake in development might allow millions of people to figure out your birthday or your scanned state ID. Lastly, many projects stress the time to market, sacrificing good code and secure implementation over speed. The problem will be better once the environment becomes more mature. 

Usability vs Decentralization

    The landscape of Web 3.0 is dominated by the struggle between usability and decentralization. The old version of Ethereum, while very decentralized and secure, has high gas fees and slow transaction speed, which reduces usability. The newer version, in contrast, has lower gas fees and faster transaction speed, but, due to Proof-of-Stake, concentrates power in the hands of a few with high Ethereum market share. OpenSea hosts NFT pictures on their own server, only pushing the link to the blockchain, which loses the point of decentralization and lowers the reliability of the system. Using IPFS to host the pictures puts you at the mercy of pinning services. 

 

What Would Work?

    Despite what I have described above, blockchain technology has a bright future. Blockchain technology is not going to bring about Web 3.0, but rather Web 2.5. The problem isn’t that the blockchain technology is worthless, but rather what we have done is not the best use of the technology. Why would anyone use Web 3.0 iteration of messaging when a centralized version is easy to use and perfectly fine? We are not utilizing the full capabilities of blockchain, and once we do, some services will endure a paradigm shift.

    Blockchain provides two key characteristics – immutability and verifiability. Any applications that require the two will be a great utilizing blockchain technology. So far, I have seen three great applications for blockchain:

  • Financial Sector – Blockchain provides an alternative asset for investment and hedging. The immutable nature of blockchain enables everyone to follow the rules of buy and sell, giving confidence to investors in its continued existence.
  • Ticketing – NFTs act as tickets to certain groups. The verifiable and immutable nature of blockchain enables everyone to confidently check and verify that you bought the NFT, hence you are part of the private club.
  • Software Security – Immutable and verifiable nature of blockchain makes verifying that software meets certain standards using NFT attractive.
